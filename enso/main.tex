  \begin{abstract}
  \noindent
  The strongest answer to a given request rarely comes from the same model twice.
  On hard software engineering a frontier reasoning model wins; on long-context
  synthesis a million-token model wins; on cheap high-volume traffic the marginal
  gain of the most expensive model is zero. A single monolithic model is therefore
  a bet that one weight set dominates every request in the stream, which the
  published per-benchmark spread between frontier models refutes. We describe
  \textbf{Enso}, Hanzo's proprietary closed model family that turns this spread
  into a product. \texttt{enso} is a learned router: it featurizes each request,
  scores every arm in a pool of frontier models by a bilinear utility
  $x^{\top}Wp$, and returns the single SLO-feasible arm that maximizes
  $\mathrm{quality}-\lambda\,\mathrm{cost}-\mu\,\mathrm{latency}$, adapting to each
  user online. \texttt{enso-ultra} reuses the same policy to select the top-$N$
  arms, dispatches them in parallel, and fuses their answers with a training-free
  synthesizer. The routing policy is the one specified in the Hanzo Router
  paper~\cite{hanzorouterpaper}; this paper is about the family it powers, the
  serving substrate that presents it as one \texttt{/v1} API, and how it compares
  to a learned orchestrator from the recent literature (Sakana AI's
  Fugu~\cite{fugu}). Our design choice is deliberate and stated plainly: unlike
  Fugu-Ultra, whose orchestrator is trained with reinforcement learning, Enso is
  \emph{training-free at the orchestration layer and learned only at the routing
  layer}, so a new frontier arm is onboarded by adding a row, not by retraining a
  policy over the pool. We report our measured evaluation from the \texttt{enso-bench}
  harness, mirroring Fugu's Table~1 methodology and adding a cost-per-benchmark
  column it lacks; where a measurement is not yet in hand we typeset it as
  \emph{run pending} rather than invent a number.
  \end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A frontier language model is a fixed bet. Its weights encode one balance of code,
reasoning, math, chart understanding, long-context recall, and instruction
following, and that balance is frozen at training time. But the request stream a
production assistant sees is not fixed: one request is a graduate-level physics
question, the next is a repository-scale refactor, the next is a chart to read,
the next is a one-line reformat. The public per-benchmark tables make the
consequence concrete. On the same seven benchmarks, the frontier models trade the
lead back and forth---one leads on SWE-Bench Pro, another on LiveCodeBench Pro,
another on Humanity's Last Exam---and no single model holds every column. A
monolithic deployment picks one of these models and eats its losses on every
column it does not lead. That is the structural inefficiency Enso removes.

The move is to stop shipping a model and start shipping a \emph{policy over
models}. Enso is a closed family with two SKUs behind one API. \texttt{enso}
routes each request to the single frontier arm most likely to serve it best under
a per-request service-level objective; the pool today spans a frontier reasoning
arm (Claude Opus 4.8), a strong generalist (GPT-5.5), a multimodal arm (Gemini
3.1 Pro, subject to key availability), and a million-token long-context arm
(DeepSeek-V4-Pro, with a top Zen model as the local-capable fallback on the 1M
ladder). \texttt{enso-ultra} spends more to buy the ceiling: it fans the request
out to the top-$N$ arms in parallel and synthesizes one answer from their
candidates. Both SKUs are a single \texttt{/v1} endpoint; the caller sends
messages and gets an answer, and never has to know---or maintain keys for---the
providers underneath.

This framing has two consequences we make central. First, \textbf{routing beats
monoliths whenever the pool's per-request argmax beats any fixed member}, and the
published spread shows that condition holds. A learned router that identifies the
right arm per request inherits the pool's upper envelope, not any single model's
average. Second, \textbf{a router makes cost a first-class, transparent axis}. A
monolith charges its one price on every token regardless of whether the request
needed it; a router that optimizes $\mathrm{quality}-\lambda\,\mathrm{cost}-\mu\,\mathrm{latency}$
sends cheap requests to cheap arms and prices each answer at the arm that actually
served it. We therefore report a cost-per-benchmark column alongside accuracy---the
number a buyer of intelligence actually pays---which comparison tables in this
line of work typically omit.

We are explicit about lineage. The routing mechanism, the SLO objective, the
bilinear utility, and the online per-user adaptation are specified and analyzed in
the Hanzo Router paper~\cite{hanzorouterpaper}; we cite that work for the policy
internals rather than restating them, and focus here on (i)~the Enso \emph{family}
and the serving substrate that presents it as one product (\S\ref{sec:arch}),
(ii)~the Enso Ultra fan-out/synthesis SKU and why its orchestration is deliberately
training-free (\S\ref{sec:ultra}), and (iii)~a measured evaluation against the same
benchmarks a learned-orchestrator baseline reports (\S\ref{sec:eval}).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Learned orchestration: Fugu, Trinity, Conductor.} The closest system to
Enso is Sakana AI's \textbf{Fugu}~\cite{fugu}, a learned orchestrator over a pool
of frontier models---Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Opus 4.8, and GPT-5.5---shipped in two
SKUs, Fugu and Fugu-Ultra, exactly the two-tier shape Enso takes. Fugu is trained
with supervised fine-tuning plus separable CMA-ES~\cite{sepcmaes}, and Fugu-Ultra
adds GRPO reinforcement learning~\cite{grpo} at the orchestration layer; both build
on the ICLR~2026 foundations Trinity~\cite{trinity} (evolutionary composition of
experts) and Conductor~\cite{conductor} (RL-orchestrated ensembles). Enso shares
Fugu's thesis---that the per-request argmax over a frontier pool beats any fixed
member---and its two-SKU packaging, and we treat Fugu's published results as the
reference point our evaluation mirrors (\S\ref{sec:eval}). Enso differs in where
the learning sits: Fugu-Ultra learns an orchestrator with RL over the pool,
whereas Enso learns only the routing policy and keeps orchestration training-free
(\S\ref{sec:ultra}), so onboarding a new arm is a data change, not a retrain.

\paragraph{Routing and cascading.} Enso's single-arm SKU is a router in the line of
RouteLLM~\cite{routellm}, which learns a strong/weak binary router from preference
data; FrugalGPT~\cite{frugalgpt}, which cascades cheap-to-expensive with a learned
stopping scorer; Hybrid LLM~\cite{hybridllm}, which routes on a quality-aware
threshold; AutoMix~\cite{automix}, which escalates via self-verification; and
Tabi~\cite{tabi}, a multi-level inference system. Enso generalizes their binary or
cascade decision to a constrained continuous objective,
$\mathrm{quality}-\lambda\,\mathrm{cost}-\mu\,\mathrm{latency}$, over a pool of many
frontier arms, and adds per-user online adaptation~\cite{linucb} that cascade work
generally omits. The full treatment is in the router paper~\cite{hanzorouterpaper}.

\paragraph{Ensemble synthesis.} Enso Ultra's fan-out/synthesis is grounded in
multi-agent and ensembling work: Mixture-of-Agents~\cite{moa} layers proposer and
aggregator models; LLM-Blender~\cite{llmblender} ranks and generatively fuses
candidates from a model bank; and self-consistency~\cite{selfconsistency} shows
that aggregating multiple independent samples improves reasoning. Ultra applies
these fusion ideas across \emph{heterogeneous frontier arms} rather than samples
from one model, and keeps the fuser training-free.

\section{Enso Architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

Enso is two layers with one seam between them: a \emph{routing layer} that decides
which arm(s) serve a request, and a \emph{serving layer} that presents the family
as one metered product. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows both SKUs.

\subsection{The routing layer: policy as data}
\label{sec:routing}

The router is the mechanism and policy specified in the Hanzo Router
paper~\cite{hanzorouterpaper}; we summarize only what the family needs and cite
that work for the rest. Each arm is one row in a cross-modal registry---a
\texttt{(model, level, modality)} profile carrying an eval-measured quality vector
over tasks plus normalized latency, cost, VRAM, and context reach. A request is
turned into a feature vector $x$ by a hashing-trick featurizer (task one-hot,
hashed n-grams, log-length, media flag, bias) in sub-microsecond time, and every
arm profile $p$ is scored by the learnable bilinear utility
\begin{equation}
u(x,p) = x^{\top} W p, \qquad W\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times K}.
\end{equation}
The selector returns the SLO-feasible $\arg\max$ of the constrained objective
\begin{equation}
\arg\max_{p\,:\,\mathrm{feasible}(p)}\; u(x,p) - \lambda\,\widehat{c}(p) - \mu\,\widehat{\ell}(p),
\end{equation}
where the hard ceilings ($\texttt{max\_latency\_ms}$, $\texttt{max\_cost}$,
$\texttt{min\_quality}$) gate the candidate set and the soft weights
$\lambda,\mu$ trade cost and latency against quality. Offline, $W$ is a closed-form
ridge fit~\cite{ridge}; online, each user carries a LinUCB bandit~\cite{linucb}
whose parameter $\theta_u$ starts at $W$ and drifts toward that user's taste as
outcomes arrive through a single \texttt{observe}$(u,x,p,r)$ call. The essential
property for the family is that \emph{the policy is a function of data}: adding a
frontier arm is adding a profile row, and its ranking then falls out of the same
$W$ without a code change. A two-tier safety guard gates every request before the
selector runs, cheaply on the hot path and escalating only ambiguous prompts.

\subsection{The serving layer: family-as-data}
\label{sec:serving}

The serving substrate (the \texttt{zen-svc} engine behind the Hanzo AI
gateway~\cite{hanzogateway}) turns the routing decision into one product. Three
properties matter.

\paragraph{One identity.} Whichever arm serves a request, the response is presented
as Enso. The serving layer performs identity injection and upstream-hiding: the
caller sees a single model family and its provenance, not the underlying provider,
and never manages third-party keys. This is what lets the pool change---an arm
added, retired, or swapped for a better version---without any client change.

\paragraph{One meter.} Every routed request is metered on one plane, so cost is
attributed per request to the arm that actually served it. This is the substrate
that makes the cost-per-benchmark column of \S\ref{sec:eval} a real, per-request
number rather than a blended list price, and it is what lets a deployment set
$\lambda$ to move spend without touching application code.

\paragraph{The 1M context ladder.} Long-context requests are a distinct pool. When
a request's context exceeds the reach of the default arms, the router climbs a
million-token ladder---a frontier long-context arm (DeepSeek-V4-Pro) with a top Zen
model as the local-capable rung---selected by the same feasibility gate
($\texttt{max\_context}\ge k$) as any other constraint. Long context is thus not a
special mode but another column in the same registry, chosen by the same policy.

\section{Enso Ultra: Training-Free Fan-Out Synthesis}
\label{sec:ultra}

\texttt{enso-ultra} is the ceiling SKU. Where \texttt{enso} spends the policy's
confidence to pick one arm, Ultra spends compute to reduce the risk that the single
argmax was wrong on a hard request: it selects the top-$N$ arms by the same utility
$u(x,p)$, dispatches them in parallel, and fuses their candidate answers into one.
The fan-out reuses the routing layer unchanged---the top-$N$ are simply the first
$N$ of the same ranked, SLO-feasible candidate list---so Ultra inherits the pool,
the guard, and the online adaptation for free.

\paragraph{The synthesizer is training-free.} This is the deliberate contrast with
Fugu-Ultra. Fugu-Ultra learns its orchestrator with GRPO~\cite{grpo}: the policy
that decides how to combine models is itself an RL-trained artifact over the pool.
Enso Ultra's synthesizer is training-free---it ranks and fuses the $N$ candidate
answers with a fixed procedure grounded in the ensembling literature
(Mixture-of-Agents-style aggregation~\cite{moa}, pairwise ranking and generative
fusion as in LLM-Blender~\cite{llmblender}, and the aggregation-improves-reasoning
result of self-consistency~\cite{selfconsistency}), with no weights of its own that
depend on the current pool. The engineering consequence is the point:
\emph{all learning in Enso lives at the routing layer, none at the orchestration
layer}. When a new frontier arm ships, Enso onboards it by adding a profile row and
letting the router rank it; Ultra then includes it in fan-out automatically. A
system that learned its orchestrator over the pool---as Fugu-Ultra does---would in
principle need to revisit that trained policy when the pool changes. We view
training-free orchestration as the more maintainable choice for a family whose pool
is expected to churn as frontier models are released, and we make no claim that it
dominates a trained orchestrator on quality; \S\ref{sec:eval} reports the measured
comparison and \S\ref{sec:limits} states the caveats.

\paragraph{Provenance.} Because the serving layer meters every arm, an Ultra answer
carries which arms contributed and at what cost, so the fan-out's price is
transparent per request---the same metering that prices the single-arm SKU prices
the ensemble.

\paragraph{What we expect to see, and how we will read it.} The hypothesis Enso is
built on is that the router's per-request argmax tracks the pool's upper envelope:
on each row, \texttt{enso} should approach or exceed the best single arm at a cost

\subsection{Routing overhead: microseconds on CPU}
\label{sec:overhead}

Accuracy and cost are the axes a buyer reads; a router adds a third the buyer
never sees but always pays---the decision itself. We measured it. Table~\ref{tab:overhead}
reports the per-request \emph{decision} latency of each router tier on a
\emph{single aarch64 CPU core with no GPU}: the heuristic tier via Go's
\texttt{go test -bench}, the learned tier via the \texttt{enso} crate's
\texttt{bench\_overhead} integration test (\texttt{cargo test -p enso --release}),
each timed over $10^{4}$ iterations after $10^{3}$ warm-up on a $\sim$1k-character
prompt against the trained arm table.

Three findings. \textbf{(i)}~The keyword-rule heuristic---the default gateway path
that classifies a request into a task bucket and reads a preference table---decides
in $\sim$0.3\,$\mu$s with a single allocation; the whole heuristic route decision
(classify plus feasible-arm lookup) is still $\sim$0.9\,$\mu$s. \textbf{(ii)}~The
learned \texttt{enso} policy decides end-to-end in \textbf{12.4\,$\mu$s}
(p99 14.8\,$\mu$s): 11.4\,$\mu$s to featurize the prompt (hashed n-grams over the
raw text, \S\ref{sec:routing}) and \textbf{229\,ns} for the bilinear $x^{\top}Wp$
score-and-select over the arm table. There is \emph{no embedding call and no GPU}
on this path---the featurizer is deliberately cheap hashed n-grams so that routing
never needs a model to route. \textbf{(iii)}~A vector/embedding-similarity variant
(cosine nearest-concept over $K{=}20$ model vectors in 768 dimensions) computes its
decision in $\sim$8\,$\mu$s, but that figure \emph{excludes} the prompt embedding it
requires---a model forward pass costing on the order of milliseconds, i.e.
$10^{2}$--$10^{3}\times$ the entire \texttt{enso} decision, and it dominates the
tier end-to-end. That domination is exactly why \texttt{enso} featurizes with hashed
n-grams rather than embeddings: it buys the same per-request signal at
two-to-three orders of magnitude lower latency and with no accelerator.

The consequence for deployment is the point. A served frontier completion runs from
$\sim$$10^{2}$\,ms for a short chat to $\sim$$10^{1}$\,s for an agentic or reasoning
trajectory; the routing decision that precedes it is four to six orders of magnitude
cheaper and runs on a CPU core the gateway already has. \emph{Routing never needs a
GPU---only the served model does.} The same holds for \emph{training} the policy:
the offline fit is a closed-form ridge solve and the online adaptation is a LinUCB
rank-one update (\S\ref{sec:routing}), both CPU-only, so an operator can fit and
continually retrain its own router without an accelerator.

\section{Economics: A Metered, Incentive-Aligned Router}
\label{sec:econ}

Enso ships to an organization as a metered product. An org enables its own
pool---the providers it holds keys for plus any internal models---and routes its
traffic through one \texttt{/v1} endpoint (\S\ref{sec:serving}). Two things then run
per org. First, a per-org policy state: the router carries that org's arm table and
a preference weight matrix $W$ that starts at the shared fit and adapts to the org's
traffic. Second, a reward loop: in-app feedback (accept/edit/thumbs) and an
LLM-as-judge that auto-scores each sampled response's quality both land as a scalar
reward $r$, which drives the same online \texttt{observe}$(u,x,p,r)$ update
\S\ref{sec:routing} specifies---here nudging the org's $W$, not only a per-user
$\theta_u$. The reward ledger is \emph{content-free}: it stores the feature vector
$x$, the chosen arm $p$, and the reward $r$---never the prompt or completion
text---so the mechanism that personalizes routing retains no customer content.

\subsection{Pricing}
\label{sec:pricing}

We meter the product as \textbf{a 1\% fee on routed LLM spend}: for a request whose
provider spend is $v$ dollars, Enso bills $f v$ with $f = 0.01$. A price is honest
only if it sits above what the thing costs to run and below the value it creates; we
show both bounds hold and derive them from the measured overhead of
\S\ref{sec:overhead} and the measured cost frontier of \S\ref{sec:eval}.

\paragraph{Cost to run.} Two components. The routing \emph{compute} is the decision
of \S\ref{sec:overhead}: 12\,$\mu$s of CPU. At a commodity \$0.04 per vCPU-hour that
is $\kappa \approx 1.3\times10^{-10}$ dollars per decision---nine orders of magnitude
below any per-request fee, i.e.\ negligible. The \emph{eval} component is the
LLM-judge: one cheap judge call of cost $c_j$ on a sampled fraction $s$ of requests,
so its amortized cost is $s\,c_j$ per routed request. With mean routed spend $\bar v$
per request, the run-cost per routed dollar is $(\kappa + s\,c_j)/\bar v$ and the
gross margin per routed dollar is
\begin{equation}
m \;=\; f \;-\; \frac{\kappa + s\,c_j}{\bar v}\;\approx\; f - \frac{s\,c_j}{\bar v}.
\end{equation}
The meter is profitable while $s < s^\star \equiv (f\bar v - \kappa)/c_j \approx
f\bar v / c_j$. Using values anchored to this paper's runs---$\bar v \approx \$0.03$
per request (the measured \texttt{enso} per-request cost on LiveCodeBench,
\S\ref{sec:eval}) and a small-model judge at $c_j \approx \$0.001$ (roughly 1--2k
judged tokens)---the break-even is $s^\star \approx 30\%$. At a sane sampling rate the
margin is wide: $s=10\%$ spends $0.33\%$ of routed dollars on eval (margin $0.67$ of
the $1\%$ fee); $s=5\%$ spends $0.17\%$ (margin $0.83$ of the fee). The judge is
sampled, not run on every request, precisely to keep it a rounding error against the
fee.

\paragraph{Value created.} Let $B$ be an org's spend under the monolith
baseline---one expensive model on every request---and let $S$ be the fraction Enso
saves by routing each request to the cheapest arm that still clears it. Post-routing
provider spend is $B(1-S)$, the fee is $fB(1-S)$, and the org's net saving as a
fraction of baseline is
\begin{equation}
1-(1-S)(1+f) \;=\; S - f(1-S)\;\ge\; S - f,
\end{equation}
so \textbf{the customer keeps at least $S-1$ percentage points} of its bill.
Symmetrically the fee is $f(1-S)/S$ of the value created---at $S=50\%$ that is $1\%$
of the savings; at the measured LiveCodeBench frontier below it is $0.15\%$. The
meter takes a small, bounded slice of a value it earns only when the customer saves.

\paragraph{Worked example (measured anchor).} On LiveCodeBench
(\S\ref{sec:eval}, Table~\ref{tab:enso}) \texttt{enso} scored \rEnsLCB\% at
\$\rEnsCLCB{} per 1{,}000 tasks, while running the frontier reasoning arm (Opus 4.8)
on the whole workload cost $\approx$\,\$225 per 1{,}000 tasks on the same
\texttt{enso-bench} run---so routing here saves $S \approx 87\%$ (a $7.5\times$ cost
reduction) at no accuracy loss. An org paying that Opus rate is billed
$0.01\times\$29.95 \approx \$0.30$ per 1{,}000 tasks, nets $\approx 86.6\%$ off its
bill, and hands Enso $0.15\%$ of the money Enso saved it. A more conservative
workload saving $S=50\%$ still nets the org $\approx 49.5\%$ after the fee. In both,
the fee is bounded above by the value (a single-digit-or-less percent of the savings)
and above the run-cost (sub-$0.4\%$ of routed spend at $s\le10\%$)---a positive-margin,
incentive-aligned meter that wins only when the customer does
(Table~\ref{tab:econ}).

\subsection{Case study: Hanzo Cloud on Enso}

Enso is not a proposal; it is the path Hanzo Cloud already runs. Every model request
to \texttt{hanzo.ai} sent with \texttt{model=auto} is routed by \texttt{enso} through
the same gateway (\S\ref{sec:serving}) whose measurements this paper reports---the
authors' production traffic and this evaluation share one routing plane and one
meter. The cost-per-1{,}000-tasks column of Table~\ref{tab:enso} is therefore not a
projection: it is the same per-request-metered figure that prices live \texttt{auto}
traffic, read off the production meter. The economics above describe the plane we
operate, not one we sketched.

\section{Limitations and Honest-Reporting Notes}
\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Provider-reported baselines.} The arm-baseline figures in
Tables~\ref{tab:fugu} and~\ref{tab:enso} are provider-reported, not re-measured by
us under a shared harness. Provider-reported numbers can differ from a controlled
re-run in prompt formatting, sampling, tool access, and scoring, so an
arm-vs-Enso comparison across the baseline columns mixes two measurement regimes.
We keep the baselines because they are the numbers the field cites and because they
match Fugu's table, but a like-for-like study would re-measure every arm inside
\texttt{enso-bench}; that is future work.

\paragraph{Agentic benchmarks pending.} The agentic rows (SWE-Bench Pro,
Terminal-Bench 2.1) are the most expensive to run and the last to land; at the time
of writing they are \emph{run pending}. Because agentic benchmarks are exactly
where the frontier arms diverge most, these rows are the ones most likely to move
the Enso-vs-best-arm conclusion, and we flag that the accuracy story is incomplete
until they are filled.

\paragraph{Training-free is a choice, not a claim of dominance.} We argue Enso's
training-free orchestration is more maintainable under pool churn
(\S\ref{sec:ultra}); we do \emph{not} claim it beats a trained orchestrator such as
Fugu-Ultra on accuracy. The evaluation is what adjudicates that, per row, and only
once the pending cells are measured.

\paragraph{Numbers are data.} No number in this paper is invented. Arm baselines are
provider-reported constants; every Enso and Enso-Ultra cell is either a measurement
injected from the \texttt{enso-bench} results at build time or the literal string
\emph{run pending}. The cost column is a per-request-metered figure from the serving
plane (\S\ref{sec:serving}), not a blended list price.

\section{Conclusion}

No single frontier model leads every benchmark, so a monolith is a standing bet
against its own losing columns. Enso replaces that bet with a learned policy over a
pool of frontier arms: \texttt{enso} routes each request to the single arm most
likely to serve it best under a per-request cost/latency/quality objective, and
\texttt{enso-ultra} fans the hardest requests out to the top-$N$ arms and
synthesizes one answer. Both are one \texttt{/v1} product---one identity, one meter,
one 1M-context ladder---so the pool can change underneath without a client change.
Against the recent learned-orchestrator baseline, Fugu, Enso shares the thesis and
the two-SKU shape but places all learning at the routing layer and keeps
orchestration training-free, so onboarding a new arm is a data change rather than a
retrain. We report the comparison the way a buyer of intelligence reads it---accuracy
and metered cost, per benchmark---and we report it honestly: measured where we have
measured, \emph{run pending} where we have not.
