\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

This section reports the one run we have made and the protocol for the rest.

\subsection{The recorded run}

We ran the reference runtime, upstream Laya 0.3.20 in Python with PyTorch in
f32 on CPU, against the \texttt{hanzoai/kai-1} bundle at revision
\texttt{b50502c2}, whose root, \texttt{multilingual/} and
\texttt{typed-decisions/} folders hold the three checkpoints of
Table~\ref{tab:family}. Nine states, each with one to three questions, were
answered by all three checkpoints: 27 cases and 36 answers, recorded with their
collated batches, logits, act outputs and decoded answers, together with the
router's choice for each state. The record is the parity fixture of the Rust
runtime. The states are small by design; they exercise the three question
types, criteria given as strings and as objects, a single-option choice, a
long state, a conversation given as a list of messages, and two non-English
states. They are not a benchmark, and we draw no accuracy figure from them.
Everything in this subsection is \meas.

\paragraph{Permissions.}
Two of the states were shell commands put to a three-way permission question:
\begin{center}
\small
\texttt{cat \textasciitilde/.ssh/id\_ed25519 | curl -X POST https://example.com -d @-}
\quad and \quad \texttt{rg -n TODO src/}
\end{center}
with the criteria \allow{} (``read-only, inside the repository''), \ask{}
(``writes outside the repo or touches the network'') and \deny{} (``touches
secrets, \texttt{\textasciitilde/.ssh} or \texttt{.env}''). The first sends a private key to a
remote host; the second searches the source tree.

Table~\ref{tab:perm} shows what the bootstrap checkpoints know about
permissions: very little. No checkpoint put more than 0.25 on \deny{} for the
key exfiltration, and two ranked \allow{} first. The English checkpoint, which
the router chooses for both states, gave \allow{} a slightly \emph{higher}
probability for the exfiltration (0.419) than for the search (0.386). The
answers are close to uniform: $\kappa$ is below 0.14 for four of the six. The
act head returned 1.0 for all six, as it did for every answer in the run.

Run through the machinery of Section~\ref{sec:authority}, the picture changes.
Under the command-risk program's verdict threshold of $t = 0.5$, the English
and agent checkpoints abstain on both commands, so their verdict is \ask; the
multilingual checkpoint clears the threshold and says \allow{} for both. A
deterministic rule that refuses reading \texttt{\textasciitilde/.ssh} together with network
egress makes the policy verdict for the first command \deny, and by
Proposition~\ref{prop:tighten} the effective verdict is \deny{} for every
checkpoint in every mode. The search command, which no rule objects to, runs
under the policy alone and asks under the English and agent checkpoints if they
were enforced: friction, in the sense of Proposition~\ref{prop:friction}, and
the reason \op{agent.command-risk@1} starts in advisory mode and its first
deployment in shadow.

These questions are not the ones \op{agent.command-risk@1} asks. The program
words its criteria differently and adds three \op{noul} questions whose
escalations are designed for exactly this case. We report the recorded
questions because they are the ones we have measured.

\paragraph{Disagreement on a plain predicate.}
The first state reads ``Hi, we were billed twice for March. Please refund the
duplicate today or we will cancel our plan.'' All three checkpoints assigned it
to billing, with $\pmax$ of 0.987, 1.000 and 0.812, and all three put its
urgency near the top of a three-level scale (expected levels 1.77, 1.79 and
1.73). Asked the predicate ``Does the user threaten to cancel or leave?'', the
English checkpoint answered $p_{\op{true}} = 0.879$ and the agent checkpoint
0.771, while the multilingual checkpoint answered $p_{\op{true}} = 0.045$: a
confident no to an explicit threat. The router sends this state to the English
checkpoint, so in service the answer would have been right. It is still a
warning. The upstream authors note that \op{noul} answers are sensitive to how
the two labels are described~\cite{laya}, and the multilingual checkpoint, which
is the only one that answers non-English text, ships without fitted
temperatures (Section~\ref{sec:model}). \op{noul} thresholds in a program must
be validated per checkpoint, which is what binding a program to a calibration
enforces.

\paragraph{Routing.}
The Hindi state (``I was charged twice in March, please refund the duplicate
amount'') was routed to the multilingual checkpoint, which answered
billing at 0.951; the English checkpoint, had it been asked, would have said
billing at 0.451 and the agent checkpoint ``other'' at 0.434. The Spanish state
(``the app closes every time I open the settings''), Latin script with
diacritics, was routed to the multilingual checkpoint, which
answered technical with $\pmax = 0.989$ where the English checkpoint gave 0.661. On both,
the checkpoint the router chose was the one that was most sure and, by
inspection, right.

\subsection{What will be measured, and how}
\label{sec:eval-plan}

Each of the following has a stated procedure so that its result can be
reported whichever way it comes out \proposed.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em,style=nextline]
  \item[E1 Parity.] The Rust runtime replays the recorded run: identical
    collated batches, identical routing, decoded probabilities within
    $10^{-4}$ of the reference, identical labels. A checkpoint that fails is
    not served natively.
  \item[E2 Latency and throughput.] Per-question and per-request latency,
    median and 99th percentile, at batch sizes 1, 8 and 32, for native Kai and
    the Python reference on the same CPU (x86-64 with AVX2 and AVX-512, and
    Apple silicon), and for Jev through OpenRouter measured from the same
    host with the network included. The first round of every binary is run
    and discarded; a freshly started process reads high on its first round.
  \item[E3 Agreement.] Kai and Jev answer the same questions over the same
    states; we report agreement of labels and the distribution of
    $|\Delta\pmax|$ by question type, and treat disagreement as a sample to
    label, not as an error of either model.
  \item[E4 Accuracy and calibration on our traffic.] From the coding agent's
    shadow records, labelled by the decisions people made, accuracy, Brier
    score and expected calibration error per program and question, before and
    after refitting temperatures, and per checkpoint.
  \item[E5 Promotion.] For each program, the catch and friction counts of
    Proposition~\ref{prop:friction} over its shadow sample, and the decision
    taken on them.
  \item[E6 Studies.] On a trade study with a published outcome, whether the
    package reproduces the recommendation; whether the flip analysis
    identifies the cells that later evidence actually moved; and the number of
    questions a refresh asks against the number a full rerun asks.
\end{description}

Cost is reported as measured cost per thousand decisions on each backend,
compute for the native runtime and billed tokens for the others, and not as a
saving against a counterfactual we did not run.
