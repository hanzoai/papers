\section{Typed decisions}
\label{sec:decisions}

\begin{definition}[Typed question]
A typed question is a triple $q = (\tau, \iota, C)$ of a type
$\tau \in \{\op{choice}, \op{score}, \op{noul}\}$, an instruction $\iota$ in
natural language, and an ordered list of criteria
$C = (c_1, \dots, c_k)$, each a label with a description.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item A \op{choice} question selects one label from $k \ge 1$ unordered
    options.
  \item A \op{score} question selects one of $k$ ordered levels
    $0, \dots, k-1$, each with a description.
  \item A \op{noul} question is a predicate: $k = 2$, with criteria
    $(\op{false}, \op{true})$ whose descriptions the caller may override.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Answer]
An answer to $q$ over a state $s$ is a distribution $p \in \Delta^{k-1}$ over
the criteria. It is reported by type: a \op{choice} answer reports
$\arg\max_i p_i$; a \op{score} answer reports the expected level
$\bar\ell = \sum_i i\, p_i$ together with a legend mapping levels to
descriptions; a \op{noul} answer reports $p_{\op{true}}$. Every answer also
carries the full distribution.
\end{definition}

The state is whatever the answer should depend on: a message, a JSON object
describing a shell command and its working directory, a transcript, a
document. A question never sees anything else, which is what makes it
replayable: the pair (question, state) and the model revision determine the
distribution.

\subsection{What an answer is sure of}
\label{sec:confidence}

Three summaries of $p$ are in use, and they are different numbers.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item The probability of the reported answer, $\pmax = \max_i p_i$. We call
    it \op{answer\_confidence}. When the model is calibrated it is the
    probability that the reported answer is right, which is the quantity a risk
    threshold should be written against.
  \item The rescaled maximum
    \begin{equation}
      \kappa = \frac{k\,\pmax - 1}{k - 1}, \qquad k \ge 2,
      \label{eq:kappa}
    \end{equation}
    which is 0 at the uniform distribution and 1 at a point mass. Jev and
    OpenRouter report this as \op{confidence}~\cite{laya,openrouterdecisions}.
  \item One minus normalised entropy,
    $h = 1 - H(p)/\ln k$, which the Laya runtime reports as \op{confidence} on
    \op{choice} and \op{score} answers~\cite{laya}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:kappa}
For fixed $k$, a threshold on $\kappa$ is a threshold on $\pmax$:
$\kappa \ge t \iff \pmax \ge \bigl(1 + t(k-1)\bigr)/k$. The implied threshold
on $\pmax$ depends on $k$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Equation~\eqref{eq:kappa} is affine and increasing in $\pmax$ for $k \ge 2$;
solve $\kappa = t$ for $\pmax$.
\end{proof}

So $\kappa \ge 0.5$ asks for $\pmax \ge 0.75$ on a \op{noul} question, $0.67$
on a three-way choice and $0.55$ on a ten-way choice. A single threshold
applied across questions of different arity is a different risk level on each.
The entropy summary $h$ depends on the whole distribution, not only its
maximum, and orders answers differently again. In our recorded run
(Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}) the English checkpoint answered an urgency
question with $p = (0.039, 0.150, 0.811)$ over three levels: $\pmax = 0.811$,
$\kappa = 0.717$ and $h = 0.471$ \meas. A gate at 0.5 passes this answer under
one definition and abstains under the other.

\paragraph{The convention we fix.}
Every backend behind \op{/v1/decisions} reports \op{confidence} as $\kappa$,
so a threshold written for Jev means the same thing when Kai answers, and adds
\op{answer\_confidence} $= \pmax$ as a separate field. The entropy summary is
kept in the runtime for diagnostics and is not on the wire. A Decision Program
writes its abstention thresholds against $\kappa$ and its \op{noul} thresholds
against $p_{\op{true}}$, and records the calibration those thresholds were set
against (Section~\ref{sec:programs}). The Rust runtime implements this
convention and its tests assert Equation~\eqref{eq:kappa} on every backend
\proposed.
