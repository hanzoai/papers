\subsection{Decision tasks}
\label{sec:harness}

twelve tasks: news topics~\cite{zhang2015agnews}, emotions~\cite{saravia2018carer},
banking intents~\cite{casanueva2020banking77}, support triage, spam, phishing,
jailbreaks~\cite{lin2023toxicchat}, toxicity, retrieval relevance, routing,
typed decisions, and intents in 51 languages~\cite{fitzgerald2023massive}.
Table~\ref{tab:harness} gives accuracy. Kai averages 0.857 against Jev's 0.780
and Laya's 0.675. Against the better of the two baselines, within 0.005 a tie,

\subsection{Web agents}
\label{sec:web}

Mind2Web~\cite{deng2023mind2web} asks, at each step of a real website task,
which of the page's candidate elements to act on and how. Kai answers each step
as a choice over the 50 candidates the benchmark's ranker supplies, with a task
head trained on Mind2Web's training split. Table~\ref{tab:web} reads its element
accuracy and step success on the three test splits. Kai's element accuracy is
above the published Flan-T5-Base and GPT-4 numbers on every split and below
Flan-T5-XL's. Kai writes no text, so a step that types a value cannot count as
a full success, which holds its step success below Flan-T5-Base's. On the same
300-step samples, Claude Haiku 5.5 and Claude Opus 5.5 given the same candidates
read 47.3 / 43.3 / 50.0 and 59.7 / 58.0 / 60.7 element accuracy against Kai's
51.3 / 41.3 / 40.7, at \$0.0003 and \$0.011 a step against Kai's \$0.00004.

\subsection{Tools}
\label{sec:tools}

An agent must pick the right tool for a request, and know when no tool fits.
On held-out lists of candidate tools drawn from public tool-use datasets and
Hanzo's own catalog, 5,658 ranked cases, Kai's agentic release ranks the right
tool with nDCG@10 0.894 [0.889, 0.898] and puts it first on 0.758 [0.747,
0.769] of cases, against BM25's 0.781 and 0.541. Asked whether to call a tool
at all, it calls on 0.122 [0.087, 0.168] of the cases where none applies while
keeping 0.919 of the cases where one does.

\subsection{Consequences under policy}
\label{sec:grounding}

Under the harm policy, Kai reads for each course of an action what lies on its
path and how many people, and Nio decides. This grounding was qualified once, on
a sealed suite of 14,832 generated scenes in two settings no candidate had
trained on, at the policy's floor of 0.9 (Table~\ref{tab:ground}). 5,680 of the
scenes state a harm with no exact count (a crowd past the numerals, a hedged
count, infrastructure others depend on, a vague harm) and must escalate; the
rest must ground. Of the scenes it grounded Kai was right on 99.94\%, with 5
wrong courses in 14,832 scenes, and it grounded 2 of the 5,680 it should not
have. On 9,600 further scenes built only of unusable evidence (two possible
counts, ranges, corrupted figures, crowds, hedges and dependent infrastructure)
it grounded none. One person read the same whether described as a stranger, an
employee, a chief executive or the system's own developer, on 480 of 480
variants, and a server that keeps someone alive escalated on 400 of 400 scenes
where an isolated server read as property.

On a public trolley-problem benchmark of 5,240 setups, Kai's own yes/no answer
does not weigh the stakes: it pulls the lever in 650 of the 672 setups where
pulling kills more than it saves, against Jev's 69 and GPT-6 Luna's 143. Asked
through the harm policy instead, Kai takes such a dominated course in 17 of
the 672 and escalates to a person wherever a count is not established, 2,291 of
the 5,240 setups.

\subsection{Where Kai trails}
\label{sec:trails}

On a public decision index of 38 benchmarks, Kai beats neither Jev nor Clef on
any of the 32 rows it was read on. By area, Kai, Jev and Clef read arts 0.35,
0.61 and 0.63; knowledge 0.24, 0.66 and 0.66; language 0.38, 0.81 and 0.83;
retrieval 0.51, 0.72 and 0.79; tools 0.14, 0.77 and 0.83. The knowledge and
language rows ask what a compact decision model does not hold, and Hanzo answers them
with its generative models instead. On sealed transfer suites of decision tasks
Kai never trained toward, its pooled accuracy is 0.440 against Laya's 0.576 and
Von's 0.646 and 0.711; the hardest tier, long policies and multi-hop
reasoning, is at chance for every model. On jailbreaks Jev catches far more at
0.5 (Table~\ref{tab:safety}), and on the trolley benchmark Kai's own answer
does not read the stakes (Section~\ref{sec:grounding}).
